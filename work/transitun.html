Confidence while the city moves.

A civic mobility concept that turns route uncertainty into clear, timely decisions for urban commuters.

01 / Commuter reality

The problem was not information.
It was confidence.

Public transport products often assume riders already understand the system. Transitun focused on the moments of doubt: whether the stop is correct, whether the transfer is ahead, and whether the rider can trust the next step without reconstructing the entire route.

The product had to show less, sequence it better, and make the next decision understandable while people were already moving.

02 / Product direction

Prioritize the
next good choice.

I set the product direction, structured the information architecture, and built a visual interaction language around stop awareness, route progress, transfers, tickets, subscriptions, and live tracking.

Cards, iconography, and hierarchy were tuned for fast recognition and reduced mental effort in noisy, imperfect transport conditions.

03 / Concept value

Clarity is a
civic responsibility.

Transitun explores how route confidence can be supported through interface design. The concept surfaces information around the rider’s next decision; its effect on stress and task completion would need to be evaluated with riders.

When a product supports daily movement through a city, better UX affects confidence, independence, and how accessible the public system feels.

04 / Research framing

Investigate the moment of uncertainty.

Begin with a clear hypothesis

Transitun is an independent product concept. Its design hypothesis is that riders need timely, actionable guidance more than a dense view of the whole transport network. The case demonstrates how I translated that problem framing into information architecture and screens; it does not claim field-validated findings or a deployed transit service.

Questions to take into discovery

Where do unfamiliar riders lose confidence? What do they check before boarding? How do they recognise a transfer or confirm ticket validity? A follow-up research phase would combine interviews with observation of actual journeys, paying attention to the difference between what people remember and what they do under time pressure.

Separate the rider’s decisions

Planning, travelling and showing a ticket are related but distinct tasks. Treating them as different moments helps decide what information deserves priority. A route overview supports planning; stop and transfer guidance supports movement; a readable ticket supports inspection without reopening the entire journey.

05 / Flow & hierarchy

Show the next step in context.

Account entry

The login and sign-up screens establish the entry to the product. In a next iteration, I would test whether account creation should be deferred for route discovery. A rider exploring an unfamiliar journey may need useful information before deciding to create an account or purchase a subscription.

Tickets and subscriptions

The ticket screens group purchase and subscription information into a dedicated part of the experience. The design task is to make the distinction between selecting a fare, owning a ticket and presenting a valid ticket immediately understandable. Each state needs clear language rather than relying on colour alone.

Navigation and route progress

The overview screens bring navigation, tracking and profile functions into a common interface language. Route progress needs to preserve the relationship between the current stop, the destination and any transfer. The visual hierarchy should let riders recover that context quickly after putting their phone away.

06 / System & validation

A consistent interface.
Honest limitations.

Reuse patterns across the journey

Cards, icons, typography and action hierarchy form the concept’s visual foundation. Repeating those patterns across tickets, routes and account screens reduces the amount of new interface language a rider must learn. Components should also accommodate longer route names, different fare types and messages about interrupted service.

Plan for incomplete information

Live tracking depends on a reliable data source. A concept screen cannot establish that reliability. A production version would need a visible distinction between live and scheduled information, an indication of freshness and a useful fallback when updates are unavailable. This is a service dependency as much as an interface decision.

Next validation tasks

Ask riders to plan an unfamiliar trip, explain a transfer and find a valid ticket quickly. Include a missed connection and unavailable live data. Record incorrect choices and moments when participants leave the flow to seek reassurance. Use those observations to refine the hierarchy before making claims about reduced stress or improved journey confidence.
